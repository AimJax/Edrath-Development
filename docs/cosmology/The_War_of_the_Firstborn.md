# The War of the Firstborn
**Document:** Working Cosmology Narrative  
**Status:** Proposed lore — not yet approved as foundational canon  
**Scope:** The origin of the cosmos, the surviving divine competitors, the Veylith, the Axiom, and Edrath as their shared creation-game

> **Canon note:** This document records the current collaborative cosmology concept. It is a working draft, not a declaration that every detail is finalized. Questions explicitly marked open must remain open until separately decided. The Dravkhar and other mortal peoples are not assigned a role in the divine competition here.

## 1. Before the World

Before the universe as mortals understand it, there existed a single primordial Being. His true name is older than Edraen, and its meaning is unknown. It should not be translated, decomposed into Edraen roots, or replaced with a newly invented etymology.

He existed in a state that had endured for an incomprehensible length of time. Eventually, eternity became boring to Him. He chose to destroy Himself.

His self-detonation was not merely an explosion inside an existing universe. It was the event from which the universe came to be. His body, soul, power, energy, will, and other aspects scattered into the not-yet-ordered vastness. The precise physical and metaphysical mechanics of this event remain unknown.

This event is provisionally called **the First Dawn**. The earliest era is already known in project terminology as **the Age of the Beginning**. These terms serve different purposes: the First Dawn is the originating event; the Age of the Beginning is the age associated with the earliest existence. Their final historical and religious usage remains open.

## 2. Countless Fragments

The scattered remains of the Being did not immediately become a pantheon. Over immense spans of time, the densest and most consequential fragments gradually developed into distinct divine beings.

These beings inherited different portions of the original Being's essence. They also inherited partial echoes: instincts, impressions, fragmented memories, impulses, or other remnants that might be impossible to interpret reliably. None possesses a complete, dependable account of who Him was or what He intended.

They became individuals, but each carried enough of the same origin to make a terrible question unavoidable:

**Which of us is Him?**

## 3. The War of the Firstborn

The newly awakened divine beings became competitive and began fighting over their claim to the original Being's identity. Each sought to establish that they were the true continuation, rightful successor, or closest expression of Him.

The conflict continued across spans of time beyond mortal comprehension. The combatants were originally countless. Their war was not a brief creation myth or a single battle, but an apparently endless struggle that consumed the participants and reshaped the reality in which they fought.

The war's exact events are deliberately unknown. No mortal has ever witnessed it. The surviving gods refuse to give a full account, and no trustworthy mortal record can resolve what happened. Their silence might arise from shame, pain, fear, indifference, secrecy, or reasons not yet imagined; no explanation is established.

Even the surviving gods may possess only fragmented recollections, and their accounts—if any are ever given—need not agree.

## 4. A Universe Takes Shape

The physical universe known to mortals emerged through the long aftermath of this cosmic conflict. Stars, galaxies, black holes, nebulae, planets, asteroids, and other celestial phenomena exist within the universe that took shape during and after the war.

The mythic account and physical reality should coexist without reducing the cosmos to a sequence of literal punches. Some celestial events may be remembered as consequences of divine conflict; other structures and phenomena can arise through consistent astrophysical processes. Stars still form, matter still gathers under gravity, and black holes still behave as black holes. The degree to which divine actions directly influenced particular astronomical events remains undecided.

The result is a real, immense physical universe—not merely an artificial arena that looks like space.

## 5. When the War Became Unsustainable

Over time, the number of combatants dwindled from countless beings to a handful of survivors. Why each of the others disappeared is not yet established. Destruction, exhaustion, transformation, imprisonment, diminution, or other outcomes are possibilities only, not settled explanations.

The remaining Firstborn faced an obvious problem: if they continued fighting as before, they risked leaving no competitors alive. Despite ages of conflict, none had conclusively proven that they were the original Being.

At some point, the survivors stopped direct, unrestrained warfare and turned toward a different pastime.

They did not necessarily reconcile, forgive one another, or abandon their rivalry. They changed how they competed.

## 6. Creation Becomes the Competition

The surviving divine beings began treating creation, development, and the nurturing of life as a competitive game. Instead of simply trying to shed one another's blood, they would demonstrate their superiority through what they could make and what their creations could become.

The contest is not fundamentally about producing the prettiest world, the largest population, or the strongest individual. Its underlying ambition is to prove who is **closest to Him**.

Each Firstborn believes—or at least behaves as though—their ability to create, develop, influence, and cultivate existence can demonstrate their proximity to the original Being. Yet no agreed scoring system can objectively establish what “closest” means. They may value different achievements, dispute one another's standards, and interpret the same result in incompatible ways.

The competition is the continuation of the old identity struggle in a new form:

- The old question: **Which of us is Him?**
- The new question: **Which of us is most like Him?**

No final reward, guaranteed increase in power, or objective method of declaring a winner has been established. The drive to prove themselves may be enough to sustain the competition indefinitely.

## 7. Edrath, the Shared Map

**Edrath is the shared multiplayer map on which the Firstborn compete.** The gods' creation-game is literal within this proposed cosmology, not merely a metaphor used by mortal religions.

The Firstborn take turns influencing the same world. Their creations are not confined to isolated, sealed-off worlds or separate territories. The creations of different competitors can overlap, interact, cooperate, compete, and collide within Edrath.

One Firstborn may cultivate forms of life and allow them to develop. Another may shape conditions that challenge those creations. Another may intervene in a civilization, empower a champion, reveal knowledge, or change the course of events. The exact methods and limits of each Firstborn's influence remain to be defined.

A central feature of the game is that creations can develop beyond their creator's immediate intentions. Living beings make choices, societies change, ecosystems evolve, and outcomes can surprise even the divine competitor whose influence helped set them in motion. This preserves uncertainty and gives the game something to test.

Edrath is therefore both a living world and the shared arena in which the Firstborn try to demonstrate their superiority.

## 8. Divine Time and Mortal Time

Time is experienced on radically different scales by the Firstborn and the inhabitants of Edrath. A short session—perhaps around ten minutes—from the gods' perspective can encompass an immense span of mortal history.

The exact ratio is illustrative rather than a fixed conversion. No precise universal time multiplier has been established.

To a Firstborn, a turn may feel brief. To mortals, it can include generations, the rise and fall of kingdoms, the disappearance of cultures, and transformations whose significance fills entire historical eras. A god may pass the turn to another while mortal civilizations continue to experience their lives as continuous and consequential.

The mechanism behind this time difference remains open. It should not be treated as a solved scientific or metaphysical rule yet.

## 9. There Are No Clean Rules

The Firstborn have not agreed to a code of fair play. They are willing to intervene directly, grant power to creations, communicate with mortals, manipulate rulers, influence wars, or otherwise advantage their side. There is no established rule requiring them to remain distant from the world.

If one competitor empowers a mortal, another can answer with intervention of their own. If a competitor manipulates a civilization, rivals can undermine it. A god may genuinely care for a creation while still choosing victory over its welfare. Affection and ruthlessness are not mutually exclusive.

There is no higher divine court established to punish cheating. The Firstborn are competitors, critics, and would-be judges of one another, but none possesses universally accepted authority to declare a final winner. They can retaliate, sabotage, and exploit mistakes; the competition has no guaranteed impartial adjudicator.

Their central priority is to win—or, more precisely, to prove that they are closer to Him. Whether “winning” has any objective consequence beyond the status each god claims remains undecided.

## 10. The Veylith: The Lesser Gods

The Firstborn eventually created **sixteen lesser divine beings**, collectively called **the Veylith** in this working draft. They exist to manage and influence the world and the ongoing competition, allowing the Firstborn to avoid manually handling every event themselves.

The Veylith may carry out tasks, administer aspects of the game, act through mortals, maintain or disrupt conditions, and pursue the interests of their creators. Their precise duties, individual loyalties, relationships, and degree of independence are not yet established.

The Veylith are not automatically a simple division between good and evil. They may be associated with preservation, growth, disruption, conflict, temptation, or other functions, but a function does not by itself determine moral worth. Their names and responsibilities should be finalized separately.

## 11. The Axiom: The Referee

The Firstborn also established a referee for the competition: **the Axiom**, whose proposed individual name is **Ithrion**.

The referee's central function is connected to the limits of mortal potential. Mortal beings cannot naturally do absolutely anything they wish; reality imposes boundaries on what a mortal can become and accomplish. Exceptional individuals can surpass ordinary limits, but such cases are unusual and do not mean mortal potential is limitless.

The Axiom is not necessarily a person who actively watches every event and issues judgments. It might be a conscious entity, an impersonal law of reality, or something that does not fit either category. Its awareness, personality, method of enforcement, and relationship with the Veylith remain undecided.

The referee should not be mistaken for a moral authority or a guarantee of fair play. It does not automatically prevent the Firstborn from cheating, nor does it require them to treat their creations kindly. It establishes a boundary associated with mortal potential; the exact way that boundary interacts with divine gifts, exceptional mortals, and the Veylith remains to be defined.

**Ithrion** is a provisional proper name. **The Axiom** is the collective title used for the singular referee in this draft.

## 12. The Eight Firstborn

The surviving divine competitors are collectively called **the Firstborn**. There are eight of them in the current working cosmology. Their individual true names are provisional and have no established Edraen etymologies.

1. Vaeroth
2. Kharzun
3. Oruvael
4. Thessarak
5. Maelvorn
6. Ithranel
7. Zhaurath
8. Velmoraith

These names do not yet determine their personalities, domains, methods of competition, moral positions, or relationships with one another. None has been established as the strongest, wisest, most virtuous, or closest to Him. Their rivalry remains unresolved.

Their collective title does not mean that mortals know their true nature or that the names above are used in mortal religions. The gap between what is true in the underlying cosmology and what mortals can know is an important part of the setting.

## 13. The Mortal View: A Hidden Game

No mortal has witnessed the War of the Firstborn. The surviving gods refuse to explain it, and mortal religions, myths, and scholarly accounts cannot be assumed to know the underlying truth.

Mortals may worship the Veylith or the Firstborn, tell conflicting creation stories, interpret divine interventions as blessings or curses, or argue about whether the gods are benevolent. Such beliefs may be sincere without being accurate. A real divine event does not guarantee that a mortal account correctly understands its cause or meaning.

A mortal champion might receive power without understanding why. A ruler might interpret a god's attention as proof of righteousness when it is actually part of the competition. A civilization might flourish under divine influence or suffer from rival intervention. None of these possibilities should be assigned to a specific people or historical event until that lore is developed.

The gods' refusal to elaborate is itself part of the mystery. Whether they are ashamed, traumatized, protective of a secret, indifferent to mortals, or simply unwilling to discuss the past remains unknown.

## 14. The Central Irony

The original Being destroyed Himself because eternity had become boring.

His countless fragments then fought for ages over which of them was Him. The survivors eventually replaced open warfare with a competition in creation, development, and cultivation. They now attempt to prove who is most like the Being whose destruction made their existence possible.

They may have inherited His power, but they have no settled answer to what made Him who He was—or what it would mean to become closest to Him.

The universe that emerged from their ancient conflict has become their shared game. Edrath is the map. Mortals live within it, make meaningful choices, build societies, and experience history on a scale that dwarfs the gods' own sense of time.

The gods can care about their creations and still exploit them. They can cultivate life and still compete ruthlessly. They can claim to be the closest to Him without possessing any universally accepted proof.

**The war did not truly end. It changed form.**

## 16. Open Questions — Do Not Resolve Without Discussion

- The original Being's true name, nature, and full motivation beyond the established boredom and self-destruction.
- The exact mechanics by which His self-detonation produced the universe.
- Why each of the countless divine fragments disappeared, leaving eight Firstborn.
- Whether any surviving fragment is objectively closer to Him, and whether that can be measured at all.
- The precise rules, if any, that govern turns in the creation-game beyond the absence of a fair-play code.
- The mechanism behind the difference between divine and mortal time.
- The individual domains, personalities, alliances, and rivalries of the eight Firstborn.
- The individual roles, loyalties, independence, and morality of the sixteen Veylith.
- Whether the Veylith are organized into two or more orders, and whether those orders should be understood as good and evil, functional categories, or something else.
- Whether Ithrion/the Axiom is conscious, how the mortal limit operates, and what it means for a mortal to surpass it.
- The limits of divine power and the exact ways divine gifts interact with mortal potential.
- Whether and how the divine competition has influenced specific mortal peoples, historical events, magic, religions, or civilizations.
- What any mortal religion or civilization believes about the Firstborn, the Veylith, the Axiom, and the First Dawn.

Do not use this draft to explain humanity's established superiority, Dravkhar potential, or the origins of any other race. Those subjects remain separately deferred.
